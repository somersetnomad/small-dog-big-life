A small dog. Not a small budget.

Prepare · UK financesAbout 10 minutes, plus calculatorFigures reviewed November 2026

Chihuahuas eat very little, but vet care, insurance and dentistry cost much the same as for a larger dog, and toy breeds need dental work more often than most. Here is an honest, itemised picture, and a calculator you can adjust to your own prices.

In brief
  • The PDSA puts the minimum lifetime cost of a small dog at £6,200–£12,000, before the purchase price and before any vet bills for illness or injury.
  • Our default budget comes to about £3,500 (adult rescue) to £5,500 (breeder puppy) in the first year, including a £1,000 emergency fund, then about £1,800 a year.
  • Dental care is the cost new owners most often forget.
  • Lifetime insurance plus an emergency fund is the safest combination.

01The big picture

The PDSA, the UK veterinary charity, estimates that a small dog costs at least £69 a month, with set-up costs of at least £415 and a minimum lifetime cost of £6,200 to £12,000. Those figures were calculated in 2024. They cover routine care only: they leave out the purchase price, vet fees for illness or injury, special diets, microchipping, day care, boarding, grooming and training.1

Real budgets are usually higher, for three reasons that apply strongly to Chihuahuas:

  • Dentistry. Dental disease was the most commonly recorded condition in UK Chihuahuas.2 Many need professional cleaning under general anaesthetic every year or two.
  • Insurance rises with age. Premiums for lifetime cover usually increase every year, and a Chihuahua can live into its mid-teens.
  • Vet prices have risen. The Competition and Markets Authority has been investigating the vet market because of concerns about prices. Ask for a price list and written estimates, and know that you can ask for a written prescription and buy medicines elsewhere (the practice may charge for the prescription).

04Food: the one thing that’s genuinely cheap

An adult Chihuahua typically needs only about 150 to 250 kilocalories a day, depending on weight, age and activity. See the feeding guide for an estimator. That translates to roughly 40 to 70 grams of a typical complete dry food. Even good-quality small-breed food rarely costs more than £15 to £30 a month. Wet food, fresh-cooked subscriptions and raw diets cost more per calorie; raw feeding also carries hygiene risks for the household, which the British Veterinary Association has highlighted.

Small-breed kibble has smaller pieces for small mouths. Whatever you choose, look for a food labelled “complete” for your dog’s life stage, and treats should make up no more than about 10% of daily calories.

05Insurance: read the policy type first

Pet insurance isn’t legally required, but for most owners it is the difference between affording treatment and not. The most important choice is the type of policy, not the price.

Covers ongoing conditions year after year, up to an annual limit that resets each year, as long as you renew without a break. Most expensive, but the only type that reliably covers lifelong problems like heart disease, patellar luxation or allergies. Check the annual limit (aim for several thousand pounds), whether dental illness is covered, and how premiums rise with age.

A fixed amount per condition, for as long as it takes to use up. Once the money for that condition runs out, it’s no longer covered. Cheaper, but risky for chronic conditions.

Covers each condition for a fixed period, usually 12 months, after which it’s excluded. Fine for one-off illnesses and injuries, poor for anything lasting.

Covers injuries but not illness. Cheapest, and better than nothing for a dog prone to falls, but it won’t help with the illnesses toy breeds commonly develop.

Insure from day one, before any symptoms appear. Anything noticed before your cover starts will usually be excluded as “pre-existing” for life. Many policies also exclude dental disease unless your dog has had regular dental checks, so read that section carefully.

06Routine vet care

  • Annual health check and booster: usually £60–£120 together. Some boosters are given every year (leptospirosis), others every three years.
  • Parasite prevention: monthly or quarterly flea, tick and worm treatment, including protection against lungworm in many parts of the UK. Roughly £100–£200 a year for a toy dog.
  • Practice health plans: many vets offer a monthly plan (often £15–£25) covering the annual check, vaccinations, parasite treatment and discounts. These spread the cost but are not insurance.

07Dental care: budget for it

Small mouths crowd teeth together, which traps food and plaque. Many Chihuahuas also keep baby teeth that should have fallen out. The result is gum disease, which causes pain, bad breath, tooth loss and infection that can affect the rest of the body.

Prevention costs very little: daily tooth brushing with dog toothpaste. Treatment costs much more. A professional scale and polish under general anaesthetic at a UK vet commonly costs several hundred pounds, more if X-rays or extractions are needed. Many toy dogs need one every year or two despite good home care. Our calculator assumes £550 every two years by default. Change it once you have your vet’s estimate.

08The emergency fund

Even with insurance, you will pay the excess on each claim (and often a percentage “co-payment” for older dogs), the cost of anything excluded, and bills while you wait for claims to settle. A fund of £1,000 or more, built up over the first year, is a sensible target. If you choose not to insure, the fund should be much larger: emergency surgery or a stay in an out-of-hours hospital can easily run into thousands of pounds.

09Saving without cutting care

  • Prevention pays. Tooth brushing, keeping your dog lean and safe handling prevent the commonest costly problems.
  • Compare insurance at each renewal, but don’t switch if your dog has an ongoing condition: the new insurer will exclude it.
  • Ask for written prescriptions for long-term medication and compare prices with accredited online pharmacies.
  • Swap with friends for holiday care, or use trusted sitters rather than kennels.
  • Charity help. If you receive certain means-tested benefits, you may be eligible for free or reduced-cost treatment at a PDSA Pet Hospital, Blue Cross or RSPCA clinic. Check eligibility before you need it.
  • Don’t economise on food quality or neutering in ways that cost more later.
